Critical Essay: LLMs as a Tool, Not a Crutch

Tool, Not Crutch: How I Plan to Use LLMs as a Student

A critical reflection on using ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot in my studies

A tool extends what I can do, while a crutch replaces something I should be able to do myself. The same chatbot can be either, depending on whether I use it to think harder or to think less. As a student, I take the statement to mean that the goal of university is not to produce assignments but to become someone who can reason, write, and solve problems. If an LLM does that work for me, I have the output but not the learning.

The risk is real

The evidence supports the warning. In a large field experiment with high school maths students, Bastani et al. (2025) found that students with unrestricted access to GPT-4 performed better on practice problems but worse on later exams once the AI was removed. A version with built-in tutoring guardrails largely avoided that harm. Kosmyna et al. (2025) reported similar concerns in an early, small-sample preprint on essay writing, suggesting that people who relied on an LLM showed weaker engagement and recall of their own work. That study should be read cautiously, but its findings fit a broader pattern. Lee et al. (2025) surveyed knowledge workers and found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking effort.

This fits what we know about learning. Bjork and Bjork (2011) argue that "desirable difficulties", such as struggling to recall or solve something, strengthen long-term retention. An LLM can remove exactly that struggle, which is where the learning happens.

LLMs are also unreliable. They can produce fluent but false statements, including invented citations (Ji et al., 2023). A student who accepts output uncritically is trusting something that cannot be trusted blindly.

When, where and how I can use LLMs well

Kasneci et al. (2023) note that LLMs offer real educational opportunities, such as personalised support and feedback, alongside risks like over-reliance. I try to use them after I have made my own attempt, not before. In practice:

  • Explaining concepts: When a lecture or textbook loses me, I can ask for an explanation at a different level, an analogy, or a worked example, then test myself by re-explaining it in my own words.
  • Self-testing: I can ask an LLM to quiz me on my notes or generate practice questions. This turns it into a retrieval-practice partner, which is one of the most effective study strategies.
  • Feedback on my drafts: I write first, then ask for critique of clarity, structure or gaps in my argument. I decide what to accept.
  • Debugging and coding: I can ask it to explain an error message or why my code fails, rather than pasting the assignment and copying the solution.
  • Brainstorming and planning: It helps me break down a project or generate angles, but the argument and conclusions must be mine.

According to Chi and Wylie's (2014) ICAP framework, learning deepens as students move from passive to constructive and interactive engagement. Asking an LLM to challenge my reasoning is interactive, whereas copying its answer is passive.

Where I draw the line

I avoid using LLMs to write submitted work, to summarise readings I have not read, or to solve problems I have not attempted. I verify facts and references against original sources, and I follow my institution's academic integrity policy, disclosing AI use where required. A useful test is to ask whether I could explain and defend this work without the tool.

Conclusion

LLMs can make me a better learner, but only if I stay the one doing the thinking. Used as a tutor, quiz-master and critic, they enhance my learning. Used as a substitute for effort, they quietly erode the skills my degree is meant to build.
